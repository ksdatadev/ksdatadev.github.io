The Virtues the Last Machine Needed

The usual story says schools once taught character and, around the First World War, switched to teaching information so people could run the industrial machine. The story is half right.

The brief

Schools never stopped forming character. What changed was which virtues they formed: piety and honour gave way to compliance and reliability.

The distinction matters to anyone redesigning work around AI. The virtues the last machine needed are the ones the new machine supplies most cheaply.

The floor

Nineteenth-century education stated its moral aims openly. Thomas Arnold, headmaster of Rugby from 1828 to 1841, ranked religious and moral principle first, gentlemanly conduct second and intellectual ability third. In America, the McGuffey Readers sold an estimated 120 million copies and taught reading through stories of honesty, thrift and providence.

The virtues on offer were piety, honour, self-command and duty. They pointed towards God, family and civic standing. A boy was formed to govern himself and, in the elite case, to govern others.

The picture was never pure. Most children received a few years of rote recitation, and Dickens mocked fact-cramming in Hard Times in 1854. Horace Mann's Fifth Annual Report (1841) collected manufacturers' testimony that schooled workers were more orderly, punctual and trustworthy. The new virtues were already named; they lacked a method and the machinery to form them at scale.

The reframe

The method arrived between 1905 and 1920. Frederick Taylor published The Principles of Scientific Management in 1911, and school administrators borrowed his vocabulary within five years. Ellwood Cubberley's Public School Administration (1916) described schools as factories shaping raw material to specification, and Franklin Bobbitt's The Curriculum (1918) applied activity analysis to the whole of adult life, turning even civic and moral conduct into specified behaviours to be trained.

The infrastructure followed. The Carnegie Unit (1906), a standard written to decide which colleges' professors qualified for a Carnegie pension, turned learning into seat time. Intelligence testing, scaled through the Army Alpha in 1917, made sorting children a scientific task. Bells, timetables and age-graded classes trained bodies to the rhythm of the work shift.

It is sometimes described as a move from character to information, but really it is a move from one character to another. The NEA's Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education (1918), the charter of the efficiency era, still listed ethical character among its seven aims, defined in civic and social terms, with no reference to religion. Character survived; its emphasis changed.

The build

Sociologists later named what the classroom was teaching. Philip Jackson's Life in Classrooms (1968) is credited with coining the term "hidden curriculum": learning to wait, to tolerate interruption, to work under constant evaluation and to accept authority one did not choose.

Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis pressed the point furthest in Schooling in Capitalist America (1976). They argued that grades rewarded punctuality, perseverance, dependability and deference, the same traits supervisors rewarded in workers, and penalised creativity and independence.

The mechanism is simple. The industrial economy's bottleneck was coordination at scale: thousands of people arriving on time, following procedure and repeating a task without variation. Information mattered less than disposition, and schools supplied the disposition.

The older virtues lost their footing for a separate reason. Their source was a shared Protestant consensus, which had been fracturing since the 1840s, when Catholic families objected to compulsory King James readings. It lost its last official expression in the prayer rulings of 1962 and 1963. Compliance and reliability needed no theology; employment justified them on its own.

The principle
Institutions form the virtues their bottleneck requires, then call the result education.

That principle has a consequence for technology leaders. Procedure-following is what automation targets first. When AI agents fail in production, they have usually followed the procedure correctly in a situation it no longer fitted.

The scarce virtues therefore move to judgement, candour and accountability: noticing when the procedure is wrong, saying so, and owning the outcome. That list sits closer to Arnold's than to Cubberley's. Self-command and honour, in working form, are the habits of someone trusted to act without supervision.

The claim is testable. If it holds, entry-level roles built on reliable execution will shrink fastest, and organisations will struggle to hire for judgement, because neither schools nor graduate schemes are currently built to form it. Watch whether training budgets shift from process certification towards supervised judgement: apprenticeship, review and real consequence.

Every education forms character. The question each institution answers is which character, and for whose machine.
